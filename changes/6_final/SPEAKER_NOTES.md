## 1 · Title

Hi, we are PeakBaleen. Our project improves Baleen, the FAST 2024 paper on deciding what goes into a flash cache. Disks are bought for the busiest ten minutes of the day, but Baleen optimizes the average. We asked whether a peak-aware selection, formulated as a min-max problem and solved with an Ising solver, can lower that peak at the same flash-write budget. Today: the task, our design, and the full results, including what did not work.

## 2 · Disks are bought for the busiest 10 minutes, but Baleen optimizes the average

Bulk storage still sits on hard disks, and a flash cache in front of them absorbs reads. Flash wears out, so flash writes are capped, and the admission policy decides which blocks deserve a write. How many disks you must buy is set by the peak: the busiest ten-minute window, shown in orange on the right. Baleen, the state of the art, ranks data by disk time saved per byte written and admits in that order until the budget runs out. That maximizes the total saving, the blue average line, but nothing in it says when the saving lands. The authors say so themselves and leave optimizing for the peak to future work. That is our question.

## 3 · Choose what enters flash, under a write budget, so the peak disk load is lowest

The task. Read requests hit a flash cache in front of hard disks. A hit costs the disks nothing; a miss costs disk-head time. On each miss, the admission policy decides whether to write the block into flash, and every admission spends flash writes, which are capped at 35.6 megabytes per second. We minimize P100, the busiest ten-minute window of disk utilization. The data are Baleen's public traces from Meta's Tectonic storage: Region1 to Region7 are seven storage clusters, anonymous names from the paper, not geography. Each sample is a different 0.1 percent slice of a cluster's blocks. Day one trains, later days test. We tune on development samples and test on others, and for pre-registered tests we commit the hypothesis and pass rule to git before running. Samples 0.7 to 0.9 were held back from every deployed run.

## 4 · Baleen beats the simple policies, but sits 14–25% above its own offline oracle

These are the baselines we ran ourselves, all at the same write rate. The first row is the paper's Figure 9 average, for reference. CoinFlip admits at random, RejectX rejects first-time items, and Baleen is the machine-learned policy. Baleen is the strongest deployed baseline everywhere except Region6 s0. The last column is Baleen's OPT, the offline oracle its model is trained to imitate. On the held-out sets, Baleen online sits 14 to 25 percent above that oracle, which is the headroom we target. And that oracle itself optimizes the average, not the peak.

## 5 · Spend the same flash writes on the busiest window instead of the average

Here is the idea on a toy example. On the left, Baleen ranks by saving per byte, which is optimal for the average; the tallest window is left where it is. On the right, the same flash writes are spent so that the maximum over all windows is as low as possible. Formally: each episode, one stay of a block in the cache, is a binary decision. Each window has a no-cache load C, and admitting an episode removes its saving d from the windows it touches. We minimize the largest remaining window load under the write budget B. As an Ising energy, we use a smooth maximum over all windows plus a quadratic penalty on the budget, and solve it with parallel tempering, 16 replicas, started from an LP solution. Earlier attempts targeted one fixed peak window; ours covers every window.

## 6 · PeakBaleen changes three things in Baleen’s pipeline

PeakBaleen keeps Baleen's pipeline and changes three things, numbered on the diagram. Component one, offline on the training day: the min-max problem, solved by the Ising solver, replaces Baleen's ranking and becomes the training labels for Baleen's model. Component two: the model also gets a load feature, how much disk time all read requests used in the last 10, 30 and 60 minutes, computed only from the past. Component three, online: the model scores each request and admits it if the score beats a bar. Baleen's bar is fixed; ours drops when the disks are busy and rises when they are quiet, and its base value is still tuned to the same write budget. White boxes are unchanged from Baleen, and no solver runs at request time.

## 8 · Offline, our peak-aware oracle cuts the peak by 13.7% and wins 37 of 39 samples

First result, offline. Here we compare the two oracles directly, before any model is trained: Baleen's OPT, which is average-optimal, against our min-max oracle solved by the Ising solver, both replayed in the simulator at the same write rate. Across 39 samples of Regions 4 to 7, ours lowers the peak by 13.7 percent per sample on average and wins 37 of them. On Regions 5 to 7 it wins all 30, by about 16 percent. Region4 is the exception: its peaks are short bursts that Baleen's oracle already absorbs as far as the budget allows. And note that the busiest window still moves in simulation on 33 of 39 samples, yet the maximum still drops.

## 9 · The gain comes from the min–max objective; Ising ties the classical solvers

Second: is the offline win thanks to the Ising solver, or to the objective? We gave the same problem to two standard solvers: linear programming with rounding, and Google's CP-SAT. They reach 13.0 and 13.2 percent, against 13.7 for the Ising solver. Sample by sample, Ising is lower than CP-SAT on 18 of 39 and lower than LP on 22 of 39, so it is a tie. We also pre-registered a test at tight solve budgets of 1, 3 and 10 seconds; Ising matched the best classical method on 1, 6 and 4 of 6 samples, short of the rule. Where the Ising formulations do well is scale: in sparse form they stay within a few hundred megabytes at 126 thousand variables, where a dense package runs out of memory. So the contribution is the min-max formulation; Ising is a competitive way to solve it.

## 10 · Deployed, the full design collapses on two samples; the adaptive bar alone stays steady

Third, deployed. Deployed means we replay the test days in Baleen's simulator and the trained model decides request by request, without seeing the future. Each pair of bars is one pre-registered test sample, showing the change in peak against Baleen; below zero is better. Orange is the full PeakBaleen, all three components. It beats Baleen on most samples, but collapses on Region7 s0.5, plus 13 points, and on Region5 s0.5, plus 9. Those collapses flip the average: plus 3.2 percent on Regions 6 and 7, so the pre-registered test fails, and plus 5.3 on Region5. Blue is Baleen with only the adaptive bar. It is steady: minus 2.5 percent on Regions 6 and 7, and minus 3.2 percent on Region5, a cluster we had never run, where its pre-registered test passes.

## 11 · Taking the design apart: the adaptive bar carries the deployed gain

Fourth, we take the design apart, on all four test pools. Each cell is the deployed peak and its change against Baleen. The load feature alone does not help, and on Region4 it hurts. The adaptive bar alone helps on Regions 4 to 7: minus 2.5, minus 3.2 and minus 15.5 percent. Adding the load feature on top is similar. Adding the new labels on top of both makes things worse. Region1 is the quietest cluster, Baleen's peak is only 24 percent, and there nothing helps. Only two cells are pre-registered tests: the full design on Regions 6 and 7, which failed, and the adaptive bar on Region5, which passed. The rest were observed alongside.

## 12 · The collapses are scans at the peak that the training day never had

Why did the full design collapse? Both collapsed samples have a scan at the peak: many requests reading large blocks in order, far into each block. In Region7 s0.5's peak window, 48 percent of the accesses are such reads; in Region5 s0.5, 52 percent. Region7's training-day peaks had no scans, so our oracle never picked scan data, and the model learned not to cache it. The chart shows how much of that peak window each policy admitted: Baleen 57 percent, Baleen with the adaptive bar 67, our full design only 9. The scan goes to the disks, and the peak rises from 39 to 52. On the right, the counter-example: Region6's training-day peaks are scans, and there the same labels help. So labels from one day transfer only if that day's peaks look like the test day's.

## 13 · What we learned

Three takeaways. One: the min-max objective works offline, 13.7 percent lower peak than Baleen's own oracle on 37 of 39 samples, and the solver does not matter, Ising ties LP and CP-SAT, though Ising formulations scale well. Two: offline is not online. Labels learned on one training day break when the type of peak changes, so the full design failed its pre-registered test. Three: load-aware admission keeps part of the gain. Baleen with the adaptive bar passed a pre-registered test on Region5 and helped on Regions 4, 6 and 7, but not on the quiet Region1. Next, we would train labels over several days so they cover more kinds of peak, run the one-shot final test on the held-back samples, and cover Regions 2 and 3. Thank you; we are happy to take questions.

## 15 · The Region5 pass holds, but 69% of its gain comes from one sample

If asked about the strength of the Region5 result. The pass rule used the retrain-level standard error, 0.09, and the adaptive bar beat Baleen by 1.16 points with 5 of 6 wins. At the sample level the standard error is 0.75, so the gain is about 1.5 standard errors, and 69 percent of it comes from s0.5. Baleen with both the load feature and the adaptive bar won all 6 samples, minus 4.7 percent.

## 16 · Baleen reproduces, with one metric nuance in the paper’s Figure 9

If asked about the reproduction. RejectX and CoinFlip replays match the authors' runs exactly. Baleen matches the authors' runs within 0.6 points when both use the released metric. The paper's Figure 9 Baleen bars for Regions 6 and 7 come from an older, unreleased version of the disk-time metric, so they cannot be reproduced exactly. Running the authors' own notebook on their results file gives the paper's averages exactly.

## 17 · Load-based admission was tried before, without lowering the peak there

If asked whether the adaptive bar is new. The Baleen authors tried admitting only during high load; it saved flash writes but did not reduce the peak. Wong's thesis tried load-dependent thresholds on the oracle and got 8 percent fewer writes but only 1.4 percent lower peak, on Region3, a trace we did not run. It also tried optimizing one fixed peak period, which worked analytically but not in simulation, because the peak moved. Our bar scales the trained model's threshold continuously and re-tunes it to the same write rate, and is judged at a matched write rate. We have not measured which of these differences explains the different outcome.
