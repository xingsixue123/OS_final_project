## 1 · Title

Hi, we are PeakBaleen. Our project improves Baleen, the FAST 2024 paper on deciding what goes into a flash cache. Disks are bought for the busiest ten minutes of the day, but Baleen optimizes the average. We asked whether a peak-aware selection, formulated as a min-max problem and solved with an Ising solver, can lower that peak at the same flash-write budget. Today: the task, our design, and our results offline and online.

## 2 · Disks are sized for the peak, but Baleen optimizes the average

A flash cache sits in front of the hard disks and absorbs reads. Flash wears out, so the number of flash writes is limited. How many disks you must buy is set by the busiest ten minutes of the day. Baleen, the state of the art, decides what to cache by how much disk time a block saves per byte written, no matter when that saving happens. In this sketch, the light part of each bar is what Baleen's cache takes off the disks. It is spread over the whole day, and the disks still have to carry the orange peak. A saving at a quiet hour buys no fewer disks. So our question: with the same flash writes, can we cut the peak instead?

## 3 · Choose what enters flash, under a write budget, so the peak disk load is lowest

The task. Read requests hit a flash cache in front of hard disks. A hit costs the disks nothing; a miss costs disk-head time. On each miss, the admission policy decides whether to write the block into flash, and every admission spends flash writes, which are capped at 35.6 megabytes per second. We minimize P100, the busiest ten-minute window of disk utilization. The data are Baleen's public traces from Meta's Tectonic storage: Region1 to Region7 are seven storage clusters, anonymous names from the paper, not geography. Each sample is a different 0.1 percent slice of a cluster's blocks. Day one trains, later days test. We design on development samples and test on clusters the design never saw, and for a pre-registered test we commit the hypothesis and pass rule to git first.

## 4 · Baleen beats the simple policies, but sits 14–25% above its own offline oracle

These are the baselines we ran ourselves, all at the same write rate. The first row is the paper's Figure 9 average, for reference. CoinFlip admits at random, RejectX rejects first-time items, and Baleen is the machine-learned policy. Baleen is the strongest deployed baseline everywhere except Region6 s0. The last column is Baleen's OPT, the offline oracle its model is trained to imitate. On the held-out sets, Baleen online sits 14 to 25 percent above that oracle, which is the headroom we target. And that oracle itself optimizes the average, not the peak.

## 5 · Spend the same flash writes on the busiest window instead of the average

Here is the idea on a toy example. On the left, Baleen ranks by saving per byte, which is optimal for the average; the tallest window is left where it is. On the right, the same flash writes are spent so that the maximum over all windows is as low as possible. Formally: each episode, one stay of a block in the cache, is a binary decision. Each window has a no-cache load C, and admitting an episode removes its saving d from the windows it touches. We minimize the largest remaining window load under the write budget B. As an Ising energy, we use a smooth maximum over all windows plus a quadratic penalty on the budget, and solve it with parallel tempering, 16 replicas, started from an LP solution. Earlier attempts targeted one fixed peak window; ours covers every window.

## 6 · PeakBaleen adds an Ising-trained peak model next to Baleen’s own model

PeakBaleen keeps Baleen and adds three things, numbered on the diagram. One, offline on the training day: the min-max problem, solved by the Ising solver, picks the data that keeps the busiest window lowest. Two: those Ising labels train a second model, the peak model, which also sees how busy the disks were in the last 10, 30 and 60 minutes. Three, online: a request enters flash if either Baleen's model or the peak model clears its bar, and the bar drops when the disks are busy. Its base value is tuned so the total flash writes stay at the same budget. Baleen's model keeps everything Baleen already caches well; the peak model adds what matters at the peak. No solver runs at request time.

## 8 · Offline, the Ising min–max oracle cuts the peak by 13.7% and wins 37 of 39 samples

First result, offline. We compare the two oracles directly: Baleen's OPT, which is optimal for the average, and our min-max oracle solved by the Ising solver, both replayed in the simulator at the same write rate. Across 39 samples of Regions 4 to 7, ours lowers the peak by 13.7 percent per sample on average and wins 37 of them. On Regions 5 to 7 it wins all 30, by 16 percent.

## 9 · The Ising solver finds the lowest peaks and scales to large instances

Second, the solver. On the same 39 samples and the same objective, parallel tempering gives the largest mean peak reduction, 13.7 percent, against 13.2 for CP-SAT and 13.0 for linear programming. With a short solve budget of three seconds, it matches or beats the best classical solver on all six test samples. And Ising formulations scale: matrix-free simulated bifurcation handles 126 thousand variables in 214 megabytes, and parallel tempering stays within 165 megabytes, while a dense solver runs out of memory beyond about 70 thousand variables.

## 10 · Online, a model trained on Ising labels learns to cache the peak

Third, online. Online means we replay the test days in Baleen's simulator and the trained model decides request by request, without seeing the future. A model trained on the Ising labels, with the load feature and the load-aware bar, lowers the peak by 3.4 percent against Baleen on our development samples, winning 6 of 8, and by 6.6 percent on Region6, winning all 4. The reason: Region6's peaks are big sequential scans. The Ising labels teach the model to admit them at the peak: it caches 41 percent of the peak window instead of 22, and the peak moves off the scan in 10 of 12 retrains. Load-aware admission helps the Ising labels most: plus 1.17 points on every sample, versus 0.62 on Baleen's labels.

## 11 · PeakBaleen beats Baleen on all 10 development samples

Fourth, the full PeakBaleen: Baleen's model plus the Ising peak model, with load-aware admission. On ten development samples from four clusters, it beats Baleen on all ten, lowering the peak by 3.9 percent. It also beats Baleen with the load feature on 9 of 10, and Baleen with the load-aware bar alone on 7 of 10, so the Ising peak model adds on top of load-aware admission. And the Ising labels matter: the same design with labels from CP-SAT reaches 36.13, while the Ising version reaches 35.36.

## 12 · On a cluster it never saw, PeakBaleen cuts Baleen’s peak by 14.9%

Fifth, clusters the design never saw. On Region4, PeakBaleen lowers the peak from 50.7 to 43.2 percent, 14.9 percent below Baleen and better on 4 of 5 samples, and 23.9 percent below Baleen with the load feature. The Ising-label version again beats the CP-SAT-label version, 43.2 against 44.4. On Region5 we ran a pre-registered test of load-aware admission, PeakBaleen's third component: it passed, minus 3.2 percent on 5 of 6 samples, and minus 4.7 percent on all 6 with the load feature.

## 13 · What we built and what it achieves

To conclude. One: formulating admission as a min-max problem and solving it with an Ising solver finds much better peaks offline, 13.7 percent below Baleen's own oracle, with the Ising solver giving the lowest peaks and scaling to large instances. Two: a model trained on those Ising labels learns to cache the peak online. Three: PeakBaleen, Baleen's model plus the Ising peak model with load-aware admission, beats Baleen on all ten development samples and by 14.9 percent on a cluster it never saw. Next, we will train the labels over several days, run the final test on our held-back samples, and cover all seven clusters. Thank you.
