## 1 · Title

Hi, we are PeakBaleen. Our project improves Baleen, the FAST 2024 paper on deciding what goes into a flash cache. Disks are bought for the busiest ten minutes of the day, but Baleen optimizes the average. We asked whether a peak-aware selection, formulated as a min-max problem and solved with an Ising solver, can lower that peak at the same flash-write budget. Today: the task, our design, and our results offline and online.

## 2 · Disks are sized for the peak, but Baleen optimizes the average

A flash cache sits in front of the hard disks and absorbs reads. Flash wears out, so the number of flash writes is limited. How many disks you must buy is set by the busiest ten minutes of the day. Baleen, the state of the art, decides what to cache by how much disk time a block saves per byte written, no matter when that saving happens. In this sketch, the light part of each bar is what Baleen's cache takes off the disks. It is spread over the whole day, and the disks still have to carry the orange peak. A saving at a quiet hour buys no fewer disks. So our question: with the same flash writes, can we cut the peak instead?

## 3 · The task: decide what enters flash so the peak disk load is lowest

The task. Requests read blocks through a flash cache in front of hard disks. A hit costs the disks nothing; a miss costs disk-head time. On every miss, the admission policy, which is what we design, decides whether to write the block into flash. Every admission spends flash writes, so all policies get the same budget, 35.6 megabytes per second. The goal is the lowest P100: the busiest ten minutes of disk load. We use Meta's production storage traces from seven clusters, which the Baleen paper calls Region1 to Region7, and replay them in Baleen's own simulator: the first day trains, the later days test. Each cluster is cut into small samples, and each sample is one test case.

## 4 · Baleen is the baseline to beat, and it still leaves room

These are the baselines we ran ourselves, all at the same write budget; lower is better. The first row is the paper's own average. CoinFlip admits at random, RejectX rejects first-time items, and Baleen is the machine-learned policy: it is the baseline to beat. The last column is Baleen's OPT. It looks back at the whole trace, as if it knew the future, ranks the data by how much disk time each byte of flash writes saves, and caches the best until the write budget runs out. Baleen's model is trained to imitate OPT. Online, Baleen sits 14 to 25 percent above OPT on our test sets. That gap is the room we target.

## 5 · Our idea: the same flash writes, spent to lower the peak

Here is the idea on a toy example. On the left is Baleen: it ranks data by how much disk time each byte saves, which lowers the average, but the tallest window stays tall. On the right is PeakBaleen: with the same flash writes, we look at every ten-minute window at once and pick the data that makes the tallest one as low as possible. Deciding which data to cache is a yes-or-no choice for every piece of data, all coupled through the write budget. That is exactly the kind of problem an Ising solver is built for, so we write it as an Ising problem and solve it with parallel tempering.

## 6 · PeakBaleen adds an Ising-trained peak model next to Baleen’s own model

PeakBaleen keeps Baleen and adds three things, numbered on the diagram. One, offline on the training day: the min-max problem, solved by the Ising solver, picks the data that keeps the busiest window lowest. Two: those Ising labels train a second model, the peak model, which also sees how busy the disks were in the last 10, 30 and 60 minutes. Three, online: a request enters flash if either Baleen's model or the peak model clears its bar, and the bar drops when the disks are busy. Its base value is tuned so the total flash writes stay at the same budget. Baleen's model keeps everything Baleen already caches well; the peak model adds what matters at the peak. No solver runs at request time.

## 7 · PeakBaleen vs Baleen, line by line

Left is Baleen: sort episodes by disk time saved per byte, label the prefix that fits the write budget, train the model, and admit when the score beats theta. Right is PeakBaleen. Orange is the Ising core: the load left in each window, a smooth max over all windows plus a quadratic budget penalty, solved by parallel tempering from an LP warm start. Line 5 trains the peak model on the Ising labels. Online, line 10 admits a request if either Baleen's model or the peak model clears its bar; kappa scales the peak model's bar to its own day-one threshold, and the bar drops when the disks are busy. Theta is still tuned to the same write rate.

## 8 · Offline, planning for the peak beats Baleen’s plan in every region

First result, offline. We compare two plans made in hindsight on the same trace: Baleen's OPT, and our plan solved by the Ising solver, both replayed in the simulator at the same write budget. Ours has the lower peak in every region; over all 39 samples the peak drops by 13.7 percent on average. Why does it win? Baleen's OPT picks data one piece at a time by how much it saves per byte, and never asks when that saving happens. Our plan looks at every ten-minute window at once and spends the flash writes on data that relieves the busiest ones. And lowering only the tallest window would just make the second-tallest the new peak, so all windows are solved together. The Ising solver does this best: against two classical solvers on the same problem, it gets the lowest peak, 13.7 percent below Baleen's OPT versus 13.2 for CP-SAT and 13.0 for linear programming; it is fast, matching or beating the best classical solver with only three seconds per problem; and it scales to problems with 126 thousand variables in a few hundred megabytes.

## 9 · Online, Ising labels teach the model to cache the peak

Second, online. Online means we replay the test days in Baleen's simulator and the trained model decides request by request, without seeing the future. A model trained on the Ising labels lowers the peak by 3.4 percent against Baleen on our development samples, and by 6.6 percent on Region6. Why? Region6's busiest windows are big scans, long runs of sequential reads, and Baleen's model rarely caches them. The Ising labels mark that data as worth caching at the peak, so the model learns to cache it there: it caches 41 percent of the peak window instead of 22, and the peak moves off the scan. The load-aware bar adds the most on top of Ising labels: when the disks are busy, it lets more of that data in.

## 10 · PeakBaleen beats Baleen, including on an unseen cluster

Third, the full PeakBaleen: Baleen's model plus the Ising peak model, with load-aware admission. The table shows the mean peak on our ten development samples and on Region4, a cluster the design never saw; lower is better. PeakBaleen has the lowest peak in both: 3.9 percent below Baleen on the development samples, where it wins all ten, and 14.9 percent below Baleen on Region4. Why does it work? Baleen's model keeps caching what it already does well, and the peak model adds the data that matters at the peak. The Ising labels matter: the same design with CP-SAT labels is worse in both columns. And on Region5, another unseen cluster, PeakBaleen's load-aware admission passed a pre-registered test, lowering the peak by 3.2 percent.

## 11 · What we built and what it achieves

To conclude. One: formulating admission as a min-max problem and solving it with an Ising solver finds much better peaks offline, 13.7 percent below Baleen's own oracle, with the Ising solver giving the lowest peaks and scaling to large instances. Two: a model trained on those Ising labels learns to cache the peak online. Three: PeakBaleen, Baleen's model plus the Ising peak model with load-aware admission, beats Baleen on all ten development samples and by 14.9 percent on a cluster it never saw. Next, we will train the labels over several days, run the final test on our held-back samples, and cover all seven clusters. Thank you.
