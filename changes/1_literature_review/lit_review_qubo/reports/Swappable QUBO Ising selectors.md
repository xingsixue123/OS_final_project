# Win on formulation, then let Ising compete

**Executive summary.** Any peak-aware selector dropped into PeakBaleen's one `Policy` slot should beat Baleen's greedy DT-per-byte order on analytic peak load. That comes from the formulation, not from QUBO or Ising solvers. On our CPU audit's synthetic instance, the peak-blind greedy left **10–12% of the greedy peak** above the LP bound at n ≥ 10k. Every repaired Ising arm recovered most of that headroom. **LP round + repair recovered 90–99.8% of it in about 1 s, and no Ising arm matched it at any size** ([AUDIT](../../ising_followup/audit/AUDIT.md)). The literature agrees. Ising solvers win on dense quadratic objectives with a few constraints at n ≤ 300 (EIM, SAIM, augmented Lagrangian). They lose or tie at realistic size with many linear rows, and unit commitment is the closest analogue to peak shaving. So the candidates below with a real chance of beating Baleen in simulation, *and* of justifying an Ising component, fall into two groups. The first is hybrids that start from the LP and use an Ising-style sampler only where the LP is weak (peak-window LNS, the contested core). The second is formulations with genuinely quadratic structure that LP cannot express linearly, such as eviction-age feedback between overlapping episodes, and possibly joint admission+prefetch options. The pure sum-of-squares peak QUBO on matrix-free SB is a required and honest H2 arm, but expect it to lose to LP. The risk that decides the outcome sits outside every solver: Wong's PeakOPT cut Peak DT **29% analytically** and then failed in simulation through peak migration ([Wong thesis](http://reports-archive.adm.cs.cmu.edu/anon/2024/CMU-CS-24-152.pdf)). The evaluation protocol in §6 is built to detect that failure first. Two of its levers need a team decision under SCOPE.md: a peak-aware tail order beyond the selected prefix, and simulator-in-the-loop reweighting.

## Only a few interactions give a QUBO something LP lacks

The slot's problem, as SCOPE.md fixes it, is to choose episodes e (n ≈ 10⁴–10⁵ per trace sample) with per-window DT savings d(e,w) over 144 ten-minute windows, under one write budget W = 35.599 MB/s, minimizing max_w L_w with L_w = C_w − Σ_e d(e,w)x_e. The selected set must come first in the returned order (SCOPE.md §1). That core is a **linear min-max knapsack with ≈145 rows**. A basic LP solution then has at most ≈m fractional variables, and the audit measured only **7–26** ([AUDIT](../../ising_followup/audit/AUDIT.md)). A QUBO adds value only where the true objective or constraints are quadratic. The notes examine four such interactions, and they differ sharply in how much they offer.

**Sum-of-squares / min-max peak balancing** is the one every QUBO arm must have. Nothing published offers a better min-max encoding than the ones already in hand: an epigraph with a peak variable t (Lucas's job-sequencing Hamiltonian) ([Lucas](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.5843)), a decision version with a bisected horizon ([Venturelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.08479)), or a balancing proxy Σ_w α_w(L_w − τ)² with α reweighted toward the argmax window. Scheduling surveys confirm that makespan in QUBO needs a guessed "pseudo-makespan" threshold or a penalty on late jobs ([EPJ QT survey](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjqt/s40507-026-00494-y)). The proxy is rank ≤ 144, so J·x = Dᵀ(α ⊙ Dx) is matrix-free. The catch is structural: it inherits the flat, number-partitioning-like landscape where **SA loses to Karmarkar–Karp differencing** except on small instances ([Johnson et al. 1991](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/opre.39.3.378)). The Lagrangian dual of "L_w ≤ τ" gives per-window prices λ_w ← [λ_w + η(L_w − τ)]₊. That is a principled form of the α-reweighting and is exactly SAIM's multiplier step applied to window rows ([Delacour SAIM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04971)).

**Cache-capacity coupling is linear unless it models eviction-age feedback.** The only verified cache QUBO with per-node capacity (Zhang et al.) uses one binary-slack squared penalty per node. It was solved at **5 contents, 2 caches** through Benders decomposition on D-Wave, with no code ([arXiv 2312.14448](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.14448)). The closest algebra is the multidimensional-knapsack QUBO, with one slack-encoded row per dimension and λ_K ≥ max revenue as a sufficient bound, tested to **23 logical qubits** ([Güney & Ehrenthal, IEEE Access 2026](https://inspirehep.net/files/6dc97dfdba01eae229daa641fac62284)). Mapping each time slot to a dimension gives Σ_t λ_t(Σ_e o_{e,t}x_e + slack − Cap)². That adds about 1,296 slack spins (9 bits × 144 slots) and couplers exactly between time-overlapping episodes. But per-slot occupancy ≤ Cap is a set of linear rows, and an LP handles them exactly and more cheaply. The genuinely quadratic effect is **eviction-age feedback**. Baleen admits that its episode model enforces cache size "only as a long-term average", and that the eviction age "varies with cache usage over time" ([Baleen FAST'24](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast24-wong.pdf)). Admitting e shortens the residency of overlapping e′ and cancels some of e′'s planned late hits. A quadratic-knapsack pair term −γ_{ee′}x_e x_{e′}, with γ estimated from the hits e′ would lose, captures this. It turns the problem into a QKP, the setting where native-constraint Ising solvers have their best evidence. **No published cache paper does this.** It is our construction [inference], and γ's accuracy is untested.

**Joint admission+prefetch is a multiple-choice knapsack with a slack-free at-most-one term.** Awasthi et al.'s H_single = Σ_j(Σ_i x_{ij})(Σ_i x_{ij} − 1) enforces at-most-one with no slack, with A = 2·max v; code is at [QutacQuantum/Knapsack](https://github.com/QutacQuantum/Knapsack) (no license) ([arXiv 2301.05750](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05750)). Güney et al. prove λ ≥ max c is sufficient for the per-item slack variant ([ICAART 2025](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2025/133877/133877.pdf)). For options {reject, admit, admit+prefetch-k}, reject is the all-zero state. A squared budget penalty adds a linear pull −2BW·s_{e,k} that can make two options look attractive, which is one more reason to keep W as a Lagrangian price (this follows from expanding the square [inference]). The multiple-choice knapsack LP is still tractable, so the QUBO wins only through the non-separable peak term plus nested prefetch interactions. The bigger problem is SCOPE: the slot outputs only an order and scores, and the class "must not modify episodes". Per-episode option choice therefore has no output channel unless the options already exist as distinct residency entries (§6, team decision).

**Same-block exclusivity and precedence are cheap but mostly irrelevant.** Conflict (P·x_j x_k), precedence (P(x_j − x_j x_k)) and forcing each map to a single quadratic term with no auxiliaries ([Glover–Kochenberger–Du](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11538)). The sufficient bounds for forcing and precedence scale with the sum of all other revenues ([Güney & Ehrenthal 2026](https://inspirehep.net/files/6dc97dfdba01eae229daa641fac62284)), which at our n would blow up the dynamic range. Baleen's same-block episodes are disjoint in time, so the only real use is choosing among alternative segmentations (one-hot). That belongs in repair, not in a hard penalty [inference].

On encodings for W, the evidence converges. Under SA, **equality-with-offset beats every inequality-slack encoding, and unary slack found feasible solutions only 1% of the time at 20 items** ([Borrajo et al.](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2026/146074/146074.pdf)). BALANCE reports a dynamic penalty beating slack on a data-cap knapsack [details UNVERIFIED] ([arXiv 2509.19616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19616)). Every published formulation above stops at **≤65 items (SA) or ≤23 qubits (hardware)**, three to four orders of magnitude below our n, so all quality claims at our scale must come from our own runs.

## Native inequality and Lagrangian loops beat penalties, but only to n ≈ 300

Every Lagrangian-on-Ising method shares one outer loop: an inner SA/p-bit/SB solve at fixed multipliers, then a (sub)gradient step. They differ in whether they keep a small penalty, whether they update from single samples or averages, and whether they need slack. Each is **about 30–80 lines on top of an existing kernel**, and the audit's μ-bisection is already a member of the family.

**SAIM** (Delacour, DATE 2025) keeps P‖g‖² with P = 2·d·N, updates λ ← λ + η·g(x) from the last sample (η = 20, 1,000 MCS per run, 2,000 updates), and uses binary slack. On QKP at N = 300 its median accuracy is **above 99.2%**, against 94.5% for the best penalty-SA and 84.8% for parallel tempering on Fujitsu DA, with 7,500× fewer sweeps than PT-DA. On multidimensional knapsack, however, **only 5.1% of samples were feasible**, and there is no MILP timing race ([arXiv 2501.04971](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.04971)). The code is MATLAB, dense and **GPL-3.0** ([self-adaptive-IM](https://github.com/corentindelacour/self-adaptive-IM)): usable as a recipe, not as a drop-in. **Awai et al.'s augmented Lagrangian** (λ updated from sample averages, μ grown geometrically) cut time-to-ε **about 10×** against a pure penalty on QKP up to n = 300, with no code released ([arXiv 2606.24241](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24241)). **Takabayashi/Ohzeki's slack-free subgradient** (μ ≥ 0, Polyak step) is exactly our budget price. Their own finding was that **"greedy methods achieved superior accuracy"** at N ≤ 64 ([arXiv 2411.06901](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06901v1)). **2D parallel tempering** puts replicas on a (β, P) grid, swaps penalties with min(1, e^{βΔPΔg}), and adapts both ladders. That removes penalty tuning, and the authors report orders-of-magnitude gains over 1-D PT on sparsified Wishart instances. The MIT MATLAB repository contains only that application, with no knapsack driver and no Gurobi comparison ([arXiv 2506.14781](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14781); [OPUSLab/2DPT](https://github.com/OPUSLab/2DPT)).

**The Extended Ising Machine has the only precise native-inequality semantics, and it fits our structure best.** Its energy adds λ_k·max(0, r_k) with r_k = Z_k·x + c_k affine. The dependent variables add no problem size, and the engine is rejection-free MCMC plus replica exchange on a 16-core i7. It solved all 100 QKP instances (n = 100–300) in 0.0097–2.94 s. The QUBO+slack form solved **only 35 of 60** at n = 200/300, because in a QUBO "flipping any variable from a feasible solution results in an immediate constraint violation" ([Akishima et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06909)). Two caveats matter. EIM's time stops at the known optimum, whereas Gurobi's 0.45–91.5 s is time to *proof*. And the simulator is private to Fujitsu. For us, each flip changes 1–3 window loads, the budget residual and at most one one-hot residual, so ΔE under hinge terms is exact and O(1). The audit's numba local search already maintains those quantities, so an "open EIM" is roughly 100 lines [inference]. Fujitsu DA's inequality and one-hot features, and Toshiba SQBM+'s penalty-free "QPLIB solver", are cloud-only and their algorithms are undisclosed ([Toshiba](https://www.global.toshiba/ww/products-solutions/ai-iot/sbm/intro.html)). **No open-source constrained SB exists.** Toshiba's own constrained demos rerun SB with different penalty weights ([Toshiba slides 2025](https://www.global.toshiba/content/dam/toshiba/jp/products-solutions/ai-iot/sbm/pdf/20250521_WSonRQC_PittU_SB_Toshiba.pdf)).

**p-bits add nothing on a CPU.** The p-bit update m_i = sign(tanh βI_i + U) is heat-bath Gibbs. Its hardware advantage comes from graph-coloured parallel updates on *sparse* graphs ([Aadit et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02481)), and our Q is a union of window cliques. What is worth borrowing is the Camsari group's "mean-field constraints". These replace dense pairwise constraint couplings with dynamically updated single-node biases, and cut edges about 10⁵× on balanced partitioning with comparable quality ([arXiv 2602.04200](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.04200)). That is the same idea as keeping W and capacities as linear prices.

**Off-the-shelf QUBO heuristics serve as ceilings, not production solvers.** MQLib (MIT, C++, sparse edge list, 37+ heuristics including Burer2002, Glover tabu and path-relinking GRASP; default run time clamped to 120–1,200 s; single-threaded) ([MQLib](https://github.com/MQLib/MQLib)), dwave-samplers' SA and MST2 tabu, and libtsqubo (MIT, sparse tabu) ([libtsqubo](https://github.com/rliang/libtsqubo)) all need an explicit Q. That is ≈3×10⁸ nnz at n = 10⁵ over all windows, which fits in 123 GB but makes every flip cost thousands of neighbour updates. PySA's PT takes only a **dense** matrix, which rules it out above ≈5×10⁴, and it must be installed from GitHub because the PyPI name `pysa` is an unrelated package ([nasa/pysa](https://github.com/nasa/pysa)).

## Hybrids win by shrinking the problem; learned annealers land near the LP

**Every published hybrid win beat a naive baseline, not a tuned LP heuristic.** Ising-assisted LNS with tunable subproblem size (Keio) fixes most variables, re-solves a 6,000–10,000-variable sub-QUBO on Amplify AE, and accepts a candidate only if it is feasible and improving. It was **about 10% better than the older LNS-V** on 300-site VRP and beat the full QUBO on quadratic multiple knapsack. There was no Gurobi comparison and no code ([arXiv 2607.05169](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05169)). Ising-driven CVRP decomposition plus parallel Gurobi reached 30-minute Gurobi quality in about a minute, again without released code ([arXiv 2602.23038](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23038)). D-Wave's `dwave-hybrid` (Apache-2.0) is the only open CPU framework with the right primitives (`EnergyImpactDecomposer`, `RacingBranches`, `LoopUntilNoImprovement`). Its QPU branch can be replaced with tabu or SA ([dwave-hybrid](https://github.com/dwavesystems/dwave-hybrid)). The lesson carries over directly. In our problem the LP already shrinks the combinatorial core to ≤26 fractional variables, so the natural hybrid is **LP → fix → LNS on neighbourhoods defined by the peak windows and the LP core, racing an exact sub-MILP against an Ising sub-solver**. At 10²–10³ variables a sub-MILP will usually win. The Ising branch has a structural edge only once the sub-objective carries quadratic terms (eviction-age γ pairs, prefetch-option interactions) that a MILP must linearize with McCormick rows.

**Learned annealers optimize the true smooth max, but their optimum is essentially the LP.** FEM minimizes a mean-field free energy over marginals with Adam and entropy annealing. It accepts any `expected_func(J, p)`, was shown on 10⁶-node balanced min-cut, and beat METIS and dSB, though never against Gurobi. **The repository has no LICENSE file** ([arXiv 2412.09285](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09285); [FEM](https://github.com/Fanerst/FEM)). QQA anneals a convexity term from negative to positive γ across parallel chains. Its README claims MPS input, augmented-Lagrangian constraints and worst-case/CVaR scenario factors, all [UNVERIFIED]. The GitHub license is BSD-3 while PyPI says "All rights reserved" ([arXiv 2409.02135](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02135); [QQA4CO](https://github.com/Yuma-Ichikawa/QQA4CO)). With a log-sum-exp over the 144 window loads, both cost O(nnz(D)) per replica step. But at β → ∞ the relaxed problem *is* the LP relaxation with a smooth max and a penalized budget, so they then face the same rounding step that LP+repair already does in a second [inference]. PI-GNN (MIT-0, [co-with-gnns-example](https://github.com/amazon-science/co-with-gnns-example)) adds a GNN parameterization of little use on a bipartite episode–window graph. Its critics found that **greedy beats it and runs ≈10⁴× faster at 10⁶ variables** on MIS ([Angelini & Ricci-Tersenghi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13211)), and that on MaxCut it is "only minutely better than gradient descent" ([Boettcher](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.00623)). The broader FrontierCO benchmark finds a persistent gap between ML solvers and classical SOTA that widens with size ([arXiv 2505.16952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16952)). Trained-distribution solvers (DIFUSCO, T2T, GFlowNet-CO) need an instance distribution and GPUs, so they are the wrong tool for one CPU-bound instance per trace.

## Real-system wins came from quadratic objectives and time-limited MIPs

No paper reports a QUBO/Ising method beating a strong baseline on a **caching or storage** metric. GitHub searches for "qubo cache placement" and "quantum annealing caching" returned zero repositories. The credible wins cluster in three places. The first is *quadratic-objective problems at equal short wall-clock*. On production scheduling with 2k–152k binaries, Gurobi was up to **17.5% worse at identical runtime**, but D-Wave's worst result after 45 min was within 2.6% of Gurobi's 3-hour value, and the authors cannot attribute the gain to quantum resources ([Awasthi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01641v1)). On pure BQP the D-Wave hybrid beat CPLEX by about 25% ([Quinton et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.05542v2)). The second is *large time-indexed models where the MIP hit its cap*. On transport-robot scheduling, Gurobi was significantly better on the 161 smaller instances. Fujitsu's hybrid DA showed an advantage only on "some bigger instances" (up to 22,692 variables) where Gurobi could not finish in 3,600 s ([Leib et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618446/)). The third is *natively Ising problems*, such as MIMO detection in QuAMax ([arXiv 2001.04014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04014)). Q2O's join-ordering "win" was measured against PostgreSQL's default optimizer, and a 2.5–2.9 s solver latency cut its E2E gain to 1.25–1.42× ([arXiv 2601.12123](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12123)).

The documented failure is the one closest to peak shaving. **On full unit commitment (44,544 variables) the D-Wave hybrid's best objective was 5.7×10⁶ against Gurobi's 1.23×10⁶**, and quality diverged with more than five linear constraints ([Quinton et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.05542v2)). Power-systems QUBOs that work keep the linear and continuous parts classical, via Benders or ADMM, and hand only small binary masters to the annealer ([Hong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15917); [Nikmehr et al.](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9677977/)). The success factors are therefore **a genuinely quadratic objective, few or native constraints, decomposition that keeps the Ising part small, and a comparison at fixed wall-clock**. The failure factors are linear structure, many hard rows, penalty-encoded feasibility, and a patient MILP. PeakBaleen's core resembles the failure case. Only the eviction-age and prefetch interactions move it toward the success case.

Each builds d(e,w), s_e and C_w from the harness's episodes (`accesses`, `service_time_saved`, `chunks_written`), emits the selected set first, and is registered through our own entry module (SCOPE.md §1). The ranking weighs three things: the probability of beating Baleen OPT on simulated P100, the probability that the Ising component beats its classical reference, and whether the candidate fits in one day using the audit's existing code.

## A fair test needs a fidelity gate and a nested prefix

The failure that sank PeakOPT was analytic-to-simulation slippage, not solver quality. Baleen itself says Peak DT "cannot be obtained from offline episode analysis", and its threshold loop exists to "offset the small differences between offline analysis and a higher-fidelity online simulation" ([Baleen FAST'24](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast24-wong.pdf)). The protocol therefore puts fidelity first.

**Gate 0: fidelity of analytic vs simulated per-window load.** On sample 0, run Baleen's own selection through `--ap opt`. Compare analytic L_w = C_w − Σ d(e,w)x_e against the simulator's per-window DT: report rank correlation, top-10-window overlap, and a per-episode audit of planned vs realized hits. Repeat for one peak-aware selection (the LP), because concentrated admission is what breaks the constant-eviction-age assumption. If fidelity is poor even for Baleen, no candidate can be expected to transfer, and candidate 3's γ model becomes the priority. Remember also that the frozen ML prefetchers still run in `--ap opt`, so d(e,w) omits prefetch-induced DT and writes; the residual should be measured, not assumed away.

**Tail ordering near W.** `converge.py` re-converges the threshold to 35.599 MB/s ±1%, so the simulated cutoff will not land exactly at the analytic W. SCOPE fixes two things: the selected set comes first, and **"the remaining episodes follow in Baleen's own order"**. The order *inside* the selected prefix is free. Candidates should therefore emit a nested prefix. Solve at W·(1−δ) first (δ ≈ 5–10%), then order the increment up to W. If convergence moves the cutoff down, the peak-optimal subset survives. If convergence moves it *up*, Baleen's peak-blind tail is admitted. **Replacing that tail with a solve at W·(1+δ) would violate SCOPE.md §1 as written and needs a team decision.** Until then, report how far the converged threshold moved and on which side.

**Metrics.** At the matched, re-converged write rate, with windows counted after day 1, report `PeakServiceTimeUtil1` (P100), P99, top-5-window mean and mean DT for PeakBaleen, Baleen OPT, RejectX and CoinFlip. RejectX and CoinFlip run unchanged; beating Baleen implies beating them, but all four rows are needed so the comparison matches the paper's. **Track peak migration explicitly**: record the argmax window and the top-5 window set for each method, their Jaccard overlap with Baleen's, and the analytic-vs-simulated rank of the new argmax. Wong's failure signature is "a new, much higher peak appearing in a different part of the trace" ([Wong thesis](http://reports-archive.adm.cs.cmu.edu/anon/2024/CMU-CS-24-152.pdf)). A lexicographic stage (hold z ≤ z* + ε, then maximize Baleen's DT saved) is mandatory, because a pure min-max leaves the off-peak choices arbitrary. That degrades mean DT and scrambles H3 labels.

**Fairness and splits.** Every arm gets the same instance, repair, thread count and wall-clock. Report both LP/MILP at equal time and at convergence, since the real-system "wins" above evaporate when the MIP is given its full time. Tune λ, δ, k, the γ scale and any schedule on sample 0 day 1 only, then freeze and report on samples 0–9. Before claiming a gain, measure Baleen's run-to-run variation in the ML path, because GBM training adds noise that OPT mode does not have. For H3, report retention R = (P100_ML-Baleen − P100_ML-Peak)/(P100_OPT-Baleen − P100_OPT-Peak) alongside Baleen's own OPT→ML retention.

**Items needing a team decision under SCOPE.md.** (a) A peak-aware tail beyond the W prefix, as above. (b) **Simulator-in-the-loop reweighting.** This means taking the simulator's worst windows after `--ap opt` on day 1, up-weighting them, and re-solving. It is the most direct fix for peak migration, and it can live in our entry module, but it may count as changing the training driver (SCOPE §3). (c) **Per-episode prefetch options** for candidate 7. The slot emits only an order and scores and must not modify episodes, so option choice has no channel unless options already exist as distinct residency entries. An in-scope degenerate version only models the frozen prefetcher's writes inside s_e. (d) Candidate 3's occupancy model is computed purely in-module and changes no eviction-age handling, so it should be in scope, but the team should confirm this reading.

## Risks that could erase an offline gain

The dominant risk is **peak migration in simulation**. It is documented once already (Wong), and nothing in the literature guarantees any formulation survives it. Gate 0 and migration tracking are the mitigations, and candidate 3 is the only candidate that attacks the cause. Second, **Ising arms will probably lose to LP** on the linear core. The audit's best Ising arm reached 8.3–10.6% of headroom where LP + repair reached 0.2–6.9%, and at n = 1k the SB package landed at 58% ([AUDIT](../../ising_followup/audit/AUDIT.md)). That is a legitimate H2 result only if the comparison is fair. Third, **the audit's headroom is synthetic**. The ≈11% gap between greedy and LP depends on the generator's C_w ≥ 1.5·S_w and its busy-hour bias, and real traces may offer much less room. Fourth, a **unit mismatch** between our s_e and the harness's cumulative write-rate threshold would make the prefix rule cut a different set than the one selected. Assert exact reproduction. Fifth, **distillation**: the frozen GBM has no time or load features, so much of any OPT-mode gain should vanish under `--ap mlnew`, as SCOPE §4 already anticipates. Sixth, **licensing**: SAIM is GPL-3.0, FEM has no license, QQA's metadata conflicts, and the QutacQuantum and QAL-BP repos have none. Reimplementing from the papers avoids all of this. Finally, several evidence items remain [UNVERIFIED]: QQA's constraint and CVaR claims, BALANCE's details, the Tabu-Enhanced SB paper, 2D-PT on knapsack, and Fujitsu DA's inequality semantics.

## Conclusion

The question of which Ising method beats Baleen splits into two questions with different answers. Beating Baleen offline is a formulation problem: any all-window min-max selection should do it, and LP does it best at the lowest cost. Beating the *classical* peak selector is an Ising problem, and the literature offers exactly one opening: interactions between episodes that LP cannot express linearly, handled with native hinge constraints or inside an LP-seeded neighbourhood search. The most valuable experiment is therefore not a solver shoot-out. It is Gate 0 plus the γ-coupled model. If eviction-age feedback explains the analytic-to-simulated gap, the QUBO becomes the natural representation and the Ising component earns its place. If it does not, PeakBaleen's defensible result is an LP-certified peak gain with Ising arms reported as honest, losing comparators.
